ASEAN SUMMIT 2019 
Diplomatic Registration / Delegate Workflow / Multi-agency Coordination / Strategic friction for sensitive data / Multi-country Coordination /
A service design case study on the operational complexity behind appointment changes.

00

problem

ASEAN Summit 2019 required a registration system The system allowed countries to submit delegate data directly to the central host without country-level validation, creating a single point of failure. This increased the risk of errors in names, roles, and travel details, potentially impacting multiple agencies. In a high-stakes international context, corrections required significant coordination, time, and operational cost.

solution

Introduced a country-level validation layer (DAO – Delegate Affairs Officer) to ensure each country reviews and confirms delegate data before submission. This reduced upstream errors and distributed responsibility across stakeholders. The system was designed with clear flows for validation, status tracking, and issue handling, enabling better error recovery and significantly reducing the operational burden on the central host.







The system involves three main roles: Host (Admin), DAO (country representative), and Delegates. Invitations are sent via email, guiding users into a web-based registration flow. Delegates submit their data, which is first reviewed and validated by DAO before reaching the Host. This structure ensures accuracy, distributes responsibility, and reduces the risk of errors in a multi-country environment.

Challenges & Solutions
Challenge 1: Design Uncertainty (Pending Brand Identity)

Problem: The project required immediate development, but the official logo and CI (Corporate Identity) were still awaiting committee approval.

Solution: Built a Centralized Stylesheet system (Pre-Design System) using Sketch and InVision. By decoupling UI logic from visual assets, I could "flip the switch" and update the entire platform’s branding across all 4 modules instantly once approved.




Challenge 2: Low Digital Literacy & Stakeholder Complexity

Problem: Users (Host Staff) were accustomed to manual email-based workflows, and the system had to serve 25+ different government agencies with varying protocols.

Solution: Conducted Contextual Inquiry at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. I mapped the "delegated management" flow, recognizing that staff—not the leaders—were the primary users, and designed the UI to mirror their real-world coordination tasks.



Challenge 3: The "Speed vs. Accuracy" Conflict (SIT Phase)

Problem: Stakeholders pushed for an "instant-send" data flow to maximize speed, which risked national-level errors in diplomatic titles and sensitive data.

Solution: Advocated for Intentional Friction by implementing a "Data Holding Area" for manual verification (DAO Approval). I defended this "slower" process to ensure 100% data integrity, preventing the massive workload of post-submission corrections.

Three things summarize my direction as a designer:

Pride, Dreams, and Lessons.

PRIDE
Designing UX for diplomatic accuracy.

I helped turn a complex Host–DAO–Delegate workflow into a controlled registration system where each role had clear responsibility. Host → DAO → Delegate → DAO Recheck → Host Review My pride in this project is that I helped design a system that respected real diplomatic behavior, reduced data mismatch, and gave each role a clear responsibility inside the process.

DREAMS
To design systems that can support international coordination without losing human control.

ASEAN Summit showed me that UX can work beyond normal business software. It can support ministries, country representatives, delegates, liaison officers, and host teams working across different languages, roles, and trust levels. My dream is to keep designing systems that help large organizations coordinate across borders — systems where UX becomes the quiet structure behind trust, accuracy, and operational calm.

LESSONS
In sensitive systems, friction is not always a problem. Sometimes, friction is protection.

The biggest lesson from ASEAN Summit was that “faster” is not always better. For a normal product, fewer steps may feel like better UX. But in this project, some steps needed to exist because the cost of wrong information was too high. DAO review, invitation status, delegate verification, need-attention requests, and host approval were not extra complexity. They were safeguards. I learned that good UX is not always about removing friction. Sometimes, good UX means designing the right friction at the right moment so the system becomes safer, clearer, and more trustworthy.
